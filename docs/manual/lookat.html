Look-at tracking

The signature move — the camera keeps aiming at a fixed subject while the slider travels past.

Normally pan, tilt, slider and zoom are independent. In look-at mode, pan and tilt stop being things you set directly and become derived: the mount computes them continuously from the slider's position so the shot stays locked on a subject while the camera orbits past on the rail. Calibrate a subject once and the rig keeps it framed for you.

Before you can use it

Look-at has a few one-time prerequisites. Set these up per camera in Configuration:

  1. Has slider is enabled and the slider is homed — look-at is a slider move, so it needs one.
  2. Find Limits has been run, so the mount knows the rail's full millimetre range.
  3. Look-at mode is enabled on that camera's tab.

Set Ref — before anything else

Pan and tilt have no end stops, so the mount has no idea which way it is pointing until you tell it. Set Ref is how you tell it, and everything else on this page is measured against it.

  1. Jog the head so the camera is level and square to the rail — looking straight out from the rail, not tipped up or down. This is the pose you are declaring to be zero, so it is worth taking care over.
  2. Press Ref (Config → Homing & Reference). The mount records the current position as pan 0°, tilt 0°.

No motion is commanded — Ref only updates the mount's internal angle offset. It does not matter what the camera happens to be aimed at; Ref zeroes wherever the head is standing.

The reference is lost on every power cycle It lives in RAM, not in the mount's saved settings, so a reboot or a firmware flash clears it — which is exactly when you are most likely to want to recalibrate. Set Ref at the start of every session, and again after anyone repositions the head by hand.
Both calibrating and moving are gated on it Start a look-at move without a reference and the mount rejects it; every controller shows the warning, and the log names the reason rather than reporting a generic busy error.
Set it once, then leave it Every subject you calibrate is measured against this reference, and the mount aims at all of them through it. Re-doing Ref moves the frame that the stored subjects were measured in, so they will all be off by however far the new zero differs from the old one. If you re-reference mid-session, recalibrate the subjects too.

Calibrating a subject

You teach the mount where a subject is in 3D by aiming at it from two points on the rail. Two sight-lines cross in space, and the mount solves for the subject's position by triangulation. Each mount holds up to eight named subjects, stored in its memory.

Set Ref first, or the mount refuses Calibration is measured from the reference, so without one there is nothing to measure against. Starting Add Subject before Set Ref is rejected outright and the log says why. It used to be allowed, and it would solve, report success and store a subject measured from wherever the head happened to sit at boot — with no symptom until the camera pointed at the wrong place.
  1. Start Add Subject (PC app, console or web). The slider moves itself to the home end of the rail.
  2. Jog pan/tilt until the camera is aimed dead-on the subject, then press Set.
  3. The slider moves to the far end. Re-aim at the same subject and press Set again.
  4. The mount solves the subject's 3D position and saves it. Give it a name.
Aim carefully The two aims are the whole calibration — the more precisely you put the subject in frame at both ends, the tighter the tracking. Pick a clear feature to aim at (a presenter's eyeline, a mark on the set).
Calibrating one subject does not disturb the others The two aims you just took are measurements, and the mount treats them as fact. It will not go back and re-interpret subjects you calibrated earlier in the light of this one — the reference and the drive scale both stay exactly where they were.

If the geometry implies a pan/tilt scale noticeably different from the one derived from the gear teeth, the log says so and names the constants to check. That is a report, not a change: it is telling you a tooth count may be wrong, and nothing is adjusted on the strength of it.

Running a move

With a subject selected, a look-at move sends the slider to one end of the rail while pan/tilt keep the subject framed the whole way:

  • ◀ / ▶ one-tap moves send the slider to the min or max end. A live arrow shows the direction on every screen at once — whoever started it.
  • Pre-aim: pan and tilt settle onto the subject before the slider starts, so the move begins already composed.
  • Switch subjects mid-move from any controller — the shot re-aims smoothly to the new subject while the slider keeps travelling.

How a subject switch is timed

Switching subject mid-move does not snap the aim across. The mount eases it, on a curve that starts and ends at rest, so the shot leaves the old subject and arrives on the new one without a lurch at either end.

The duration comes from how far the camera actually has to turn, so a nudge between two people standing together does not take as long as a sweep across the room. Above about 58° it grows faster than that, because the axis has a top speed and a smooth curve of a given peak covers less ground than running flat out — so a big turn takes longer rather than going faster:

Pan and tilt are eased on the same clock, so they arrive together and the move reads as one arc rather than two axis motions finishing at different times. Switching with the slider parked is a different move — there is no travel to ease across, so the camera simply re-aims at the active pan/tilt speed — but both axes are still timed to land together, the same way a recalled position is. If you switch again while a switch is still running, the new one starts from where the camera is actually pointing — it does not snap back to the subject it was leaving.

The move ends when the shot arrives, not when the rail does Switch subject near the end of a rail move and the slider can reach the limit while the camera is still turning. Pan and tilt carry on and finish on the new subject before the move ends — the rail simply stops first.

This is the panel case: the slider is tracking one speaker, somebody else starts talking, and the shot has to land on them wherever the rail happens to have got to. Ending on the rail would leave the camera pointing between two people.
If a switch looks wrong The two things worth checking are the slider angle — a rail that climbs but is configured as level will aim increasingly wrong along its length — and whether Set Ref was done this session. Both show up as tracking that is subtly off rather than as an obvious failure.
Look-at vs. CV tracking Look-at follows a calibrated point in space — precise, repeatable, and it needs no video feed. CV tracking follows what the camera actually sees. Look-at is ideal when the subject holds a spot (a lectern, a set mark); CV is better for a subject that wanders.
