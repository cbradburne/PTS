# The hub's ESP-NOW transmit wedge is NO_MEM, and nothing could see it

Caught in the act on the rig, 2026-09-12 19:49, after roughly five weeks in which
every occurrence had looked — from every counter that leaves the hub — like
nothing was wrong at all.

This is the write-up for anyone reading their own logs and finding the same
shape: mounts that report health but stop taking commands, while every hub-side
number stays flat.

## The signature

```
14:45 - 17:28    cam1  0/81    cam2  0/80    cam3  0/78      direct
                 cam4  58/58   cam5  167/167 (100%)          via a satellite

19:49 - 21:16    the same, again
```

Three mounts on the hub's own radio take **zero** commands for hours. The two
reached over Ethernet through a satellite run at 100% throughout. The hub keeps
accepting bytes from the PC, keeps relaying, keeps answering its own diagnostics.

Meanwhile, on the hub itself, for the whole of it:

```
txfail       23, flat — not one increment
heap         154-163k, normal
loop         10-20 ms worst pass
leak floor   steady
overflow     0
held back    0
self-rescue  never fired
```

Both episodes ended because a person rebooted the hub. So did the fourteen-hour
outage on 2026-09-08.

## Why every counter lied

`esp_now_send()` was returning `ESP_ERR_ESPNOW_NO_MEM`. **A refused send never
becomes a transmission**, so it cannot appear in a counter of sends that failed
on air — which is exactly what `txfail` is. The frame never reaches the radio,
nothing is outstanding, no callback is owed, and every instrument built to watch
the transmit path reports a healthy one.

The hub's firmware had detected this all along. It printed a line about it:

```
[ESPNOW] send to mount %d REJECTED: %s
```

to `Serial`, on a box that lives in an enclosure. The comment above it read
*"this is the line that would identify the wedge"*. Nobody had ever read it.

## Why the self-rescue ladder stayed silent

Three rungs, and the fault slips between all of them:

| rung | armed by | why it missed |
|---|---|---|
| peer refresh | 4 consecutive send **failures** | a refused send produces none |
| ESP-NOW reinit, WiFi bounce, restart | a run of send **failures** | as above — `txfail` flat at 23 |
| callback-stall clock | sends accepted, nothing returning | the pool is already empty, so nothing is outstanding and the clock never starts |

The third was added after the 2026-09-08 outage and covers the state *before*
this one: sends taken but not completing. Once the pool empties, `in_flight`
sits at its floor and there is nothing left to be waiting on.

## What was measured, once it could be

Rejections now reach the clients as an event carrying the error **name**, because
the name is the diagnosis — `NO_MEM` is an exhausted pool, `NOT_FOUND` is a peer
entry that has vanished and a refresh fixes it, `IF` is the wrong interface.
Within hours:

```
21:07:19  ESP-NOW REFUSED a send to cam2 — NO_MEM (+91 since the last report)
21:07:22  ESP-NOW REFUSED a send to cam3 — NO_MEM (+102 since the last report)
21:07:46  ESP-NOW REFUSED a send to cam1 — NO_MEM (+116 since the last report)
```

About 100 refusals per mount per 30 seconds, continuously, on all three directly
radioed mounts. 444 events in the first episode.

## Only a reboot clears it

The mounts do not lack a recovery path — they have one and it runs. Through the
2 h 40 m episode, cam1 fired roughly **230 ESP-NOW stack reinits**, one every 45
seconds, and logged thousands of failed sends. cam2 and cam3 did the same. None
of it made any difference.

One hub reboot cleared it in seconds, every time. That matches the 2026-08-11
finding from the other end: 99 consecutive `esp_wifi_stop()/start()` cycles on a
mount left its failure rate unchanged, and a single chip reboot cleared it for
fourteen hours at identical signal.

## The mitigation

A fourth rung, armed on the refusal itself. First `NO_MEM` stamps a clock, **any
accepted send clears it**, and three seconds without the driver taking a single
frame drops into the existing ladder — reinit at 6 s, WiFi bounce at 14 s,
restart at 25 s.

Two properties make it safe to reboot a production hub on, and both are pinned by
a test:

- **`NO_MEM` only.** `NOT_FOUND` is one unbound peer and `IF` is a configuration
  mistake. Neither is cured by a reboot, and a hub that reboots over them is
  worse than the fault.
- **Any accepted send clears the clock.** The pool is one global resource, so a
  single frame taken proves it is not exhausted. This is what stops a queue that
  briefly fills — 30–90 ms to drain, measured — from ever reaching three seconds.

A consequence worth stating: **the rung cannot be tested on demand.** Forcing
`NO_MEM` with an uncapped burst produces a queue that drains in under a tenth of
a second, so the clock clears long before the threshold. The same property that
makes the threshold safe makes it unprovable by anything but the real fault.

## Reading your own logs for this

The discriminator is the route. A mount reached through a satellite answers over
Ethernet and its reply never touches the hub's radio — so:

- **several direct mounts silent at once + a relayed one answering** = the hub's
  radio specifically, and no mount needs touching
- **one direct mount silent** = ambiguous, and equally that mount's own radio
- **a relayed mount silent** = look at its relay, not at the mount

That third case cost real trips before it was written down: the verdict blamed
the mount and recommended a power cycle — a truss climb, a re-home and a full
recalibration — for a mount whose relay was refusing every frame it was offered
and which recovered untouched when that relay restarted.

## Still unknown

**Why the pool exhausts.** We know what fails, how to see it, and how to recover
from it in seconds instead of hours. The mechanism that empties the pool in the
first place is not established, and roughly 140 hours of two-board bench work
never once reproduced it — see `2026-09-13-bench-verdict.md`.
